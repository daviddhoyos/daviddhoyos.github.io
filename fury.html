The starting point

One platform under every Mercado Libre business

Mercado Libre is the largest e-commerce company in Latin America. Fury is the platform underneath it: built in-house since 2015, it’s where engineers create, deploy, manage and monitor their applications, every day.

Each part of Fury belongs to a squad. As part of the design team, I worked with those squads on new features and UX fixes, so the work below was shaped together with engineers, product managers and other designers.

Segments

One service, set up differently for each country and company

Mercado Libre runs in many countries and through several companies, and the same service often needs different settings in each. Segments split a service by segments such as Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Mex Bank or Non prod, across tools like Key Value Store, BigQueue and traffic authorizations.

Decision 1 of 5

Choose the segments when the service is created

The situation

A segment changes where a service runs and which rules apply to it, so it can’t be an afterthought hidden in settings.

What we did

Segmentation became its own block in the creation form, right after the basic details. Picking a non-productive segment sets the related parameters to Test automatically, and the form says so.

Why it mattered

Teams decide the most consequential setting first, with the defaults that keep test segments away from production values.

Decision 2 of 5

Combined by default, split when a segment needs it

The situation

Most settings are the same in every segment. Asking for each value once per segment would make the form several times longer.

What we did

Criticality, tags, rate limit and time to live show one row for all segments. A Combined and Split switch opens a row per segment only when one of them needs a different value.

Why it mattered

The common case stays short, and the exception is one click away instead of a separate flow.

Decision 3 of 5

A name that says which segment you’re looking at

The situation

Lists can hold the same service many times, once per segment. Without a convention, rows that look identical point to different infrastructure.

What we did

We defined one rule: the service name followed by the segment. While configuring, the final name appears as a read-only field, so people see it before it exists.

Why it mattered

Engineers can tell segments apart in a table, a log or a URL without opening each item.

Decision 4 of 5

Ask which segment before a destructive action

The situation

Flushing a topic deletes its scheduled messages. In a segmented service, doing it everywhere by mistake is a production incident.

What we did

The flush dialog asks for the segment first, warns when productive scopes might still use the service, and requires typing a confirmation before the button turns on.

Why it mattered

The riskiest action in the screen became the one with the most friction, and only there.

Decision 5 of 5

Access between segments, made explicit

The situation

Applications in one segment sometimes need to call services in another, and those permissions have to be visible and reviewable.

What we did

Traffic authorization separates the access groups a team exposes from the ones it’s been granted. Each group shows its rule type, such as inter-segment, with the segment of origin and destination.

Why it mattered

Cross-segment access stops being a hidden exception in a config file and becomes something a team can read, approve and revoke.

Deployment pipelines

Pipelines built from modules

A pipeline is a sequence of modules: deploy, wait, swap traffic, roll back and finish. Each deployment module lists its scopes with their version, configuration release and strategy, such as canary or blue-green.

Scotia

Andes, adapted for Fury

Andes is Mercado Libre’s design system. Fury needed pieces it didn’t cover yet, like dense tables, technical icons and empty states for developer tools, so the team built Scotia, a library on top of Andes.

I didn’t create Scotia. Like every designer on the team, I kept it in step with Andes and added the components my projects needed, so the next person could reuse them.

  • Tables

    Presets with 5, 10 and 15 rows, search and pagination, and expandable rows for nested data.

  • Empty states

    Four sizes and 16 illustrations, so an empty screen explains what goes there and how to start.

  • Icons

    Technical icons like pull request, fork, merge and version, and logos for tools such as GitHub, Datadog, AWS and GCP.
