Policy Manager at Diligent

Stopping enterprise clients from walking away

Policy Manager is compliance software used by companies like Salesforce and Cisco. It crashed often, looked outdated, and its biggest clients were ready to leave. As the sole product designer, I stabilized it and redesigned the experience.

At a glance

The problem

Compliance software used by companies like Salesforce and Cisco. It crashed often, didn't follow Diligent's design system, and its largest clients were ready to leave.

What I did

As the sole designer, I made failures safe first, then restructured the review cycle and integrated Word and Google Docs, so authors could keep drafting where they already worked.

The outcome

2x development velocity and long-term renewals from the clients at risk. The core patterns still ship in Diligent's product in 2026.

The source of truth for enterprise compliance

Policy Manager is where organizations write, review, approve and distribute their compliance policies, from companies like Salesforce and Cisco to major US school districts.

It was one of three products in Diligent’s Mission-Driven Organizations group, and the one with the largest clients. Diligent had just acquired it: it didn’t follow the design system and broke often. I joined as its sole product designer and owned the experience end to end.

  • Editors

    HR and compliance teams. Power users who live in the tool every day, drafting and updating policies.

  • Approvers

    Board members and senior executives. They have little patience for software and need to review and approve with zero friction, often from an iPad.

  • The business

    The largest clients in Diligent’s Mission-Driven Organizations group, and some of them were threatening to leave.

The problem

A product customers relied on, and were ready to leave

  • Churn risk

    High-value customers were threatening to leave over a product they saw as unstable and broken.

  • Technical fragility

    Critical flows crashed mid-task and showed raw code errors, which caused panic and a steady stream of support tickets.

  • Adoption barrier

    The built-in text editor was so limited that users drafted offline in Word, breaking the single source of truth.

How might we stabilize a newly acquired platform to stop enterprise churn, while reinventing the experience for both power users and low-tech approvers?

The approach

Five decisions that changed the product

I couldn’t rewrite the backend right away, so I started with what users felt first: the failures. Then I fixed the structure, and finally the devices.

Decision 1 of 5

Design for failure before redesigning the UI

The situation

The backend couldn’t be rewritten right away, and every crash showed users raw red and yellow code errors in the middle of a task.

What I did

I replaced them with a standardized error library. With a UX writer borrowed from another team, I wrote messages that explain what happened and what is safe, like “We saved your draft locally before the connection was lost.”

The trade-off

It didn’t fix the root cause, but it turned each system failure into a managed moment while engineering stabilized the platform.

Why it mattered

Enterprise clients judge stability by what they see when something fails. Messages that explain what happened and protect the user’s work turn a crash into a recoverable moment. For the business, it was the fastest way to calm the accounts at risk and cut crash-related support tickets while engineering fixed the backend.

Decision 2 of 5

Turn a local fix into a company standard

The situation

Auditing the errors showed that the same gap existed across the whole Diligent suite.

What I did

I audited error, empty and 404 states across Diligent’s products, defined a global standard and contributed the components back to the central design system.

Why it mattered

With one standard, every Diligent team solves errors and empty states once instead of reinventing them. Clients who use several Diligent products get the same behavior everywhere, which matters as Diligent sells its products together as one platform.

Decision 3 of 5

Replace a fake wizard with flexible tabs

The situation

The review cycle was forced into a step-by-step wizard, but reviewers constantly move back and forth between a policy’s history and its draft.

What I did

I took the wizard apart and designed a tab-based layout that lets users jump freely between History and Edit, matching how they actually work.

Why it mattered

Reviewing is not a linear task: reviewers compare the draft with its history and references, then come back to approve. Tabs match that mental model and keep approval one step away. For the business, review cycles that don’t stall mean policies get approved on time, and that is what keeps clients audit-ready.

Decision 4 of 5

Integrate with Word instead of competing with it

The situation

Users already drafted in Microsoft Word and Google Docs. Building an editor good enough to replace them would have taken significant engineering resources.

What I did

I championed an integration strategy and designed import and sync flows for Microsoft Word and Google Docs. Both shipped, so users kept the tools they already knew and Policy Manager stayed the source of truth.

The trade-off

Instead of competing with Word feature by feature, we kept a simpler built-in editor for quick edits and let Word and Google Docs handle heavy drafting.

Why it mattered

Authors already knew Word and Google Docs, and asking them to switch was the real barrier. Meeting them there removed the reason to draft offline and kept versions and audit trail inside Policy Manager. For the business, it avoided building and maintaining a full editor, and it made onboarding easier: new clients could bring their existing documents as they were.

Decision 5 of 5

Give the board its own approval mode

The situation

Executives reviewing policies on iPads met broken layouts.

What I did

I designed Policy Manager’s first responsive framework and a dedicated approval mode with large touch targets. A board member can now approve a policy in 3 taps.

Why it mattered

Approvers use the product the least, but they can block the whole cycle. Designing for them with large touch targets and only the essential actions removes the last bottleneck. For the business, board members are the most senior people at each client, so their experience shapes how the client sees the product at renewal time.

The redesign

A stable product that clients trust again

The redesigned Policy Manager, rebuilt on Lens, Diligent’s new design system, with a responsive layout that works from the desk to the boardroom iPad.

Screen by screen

How the main screens changed, for readers who want the detail.

Document details

Labels above fields and grouped sections turn a long form into something people can scan, and the document’s status is visible at a glance.

Document editor

The editor shows the policy as it will be published, with its outline beside it, so authors can check the structure while they write.

History viewer

Each revision lists what was added and removed, so reviewers see what changed without comparing two versions by eye.

Two new capabilities

Sharing policies with subscribing school districts

Some clients are partner organizations that maintain policies for groups of school districts. As they moved from BoardDocs to Diligent Community, they needed to publish new or updated policies to those districts straight from Policy Manager, which made this feature critical to keeping them. I designed Subscriber Management: admins add a district from the Community site, choose which policy folders it receives and manage its status.

Downloading a policy with its changes highlighted

Audits often ask for proof of what changed between versions. From the History viewer, users choose which versions to compare and download a PDF with additions and deletions highlighted, so they can share it with people who don’t use Policy Manager.

Still in the product today

Diligent’s public demo (2026) shows which decisions held up, and one that evolved in a way that confirms the reasoning behind them.

  • Decision 3 held up

    Review is still a tab next to the document, not a wizard, and the flow still carries the name of Lens, the design system it was built on. Reviewers keep moving between draft, history and references in any order.

  • Decision 4 held up

    Editing still happens in Word, inside Policy Manager, while versions and audit trail stay in the product. Diligent now sells the feature in those same terms. We shipped Word and Google Docs; the demo shows Word.

  • What evolved

    Creating a policy is still a three-step wizard, and that fits: creation is linear, review isn’t. It’s the same principle applied to a different task: match the structure to the way people actually work.

Looking back

  • What worked

    Fixing the experience of failure first bought the team time and trust while the backend was being stabilized.

  • What I’d do differently

    Track error rates and support tickets from day one, to show the impact of defensive design in numbers.

  • What I’d measure next

    How often board members approve from the iPad approval mode compared with desktop.
