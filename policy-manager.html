Policy Manager at Diligent

Saving a product its biggest clients were ready to leave

Policy Manager is where companies like Salesforce and Cisco write and approve their compliance policies. When Diligent acquired it, it crashed often and its biggest clients were ready to leave. As the sole product designer, I made its failures safe, then redesigned how policies get reviewed and approved.

The starting point

A product customers relied on, and were ready to leave

Policy Manager is where organizations write, review, approve and distribute their compliance policies, from companies like Salesforce and Cisco to large US school districts. It was one of four products in Diligent’s Mission-Driven Organizations group, alongside BoardDocs, BoardEffect and Community, and the one with the largest clients.

Diligent had just acquired it. It didn’t follow the design system and broke often. Each product in the group had its own designer under one design manager; I was Policy Manager’s and owned the experience end to end.

  • Clients ready to leave

    High-value customers saw the product as unstable and were threatening to leave.

  • Crashes mid-task

    Critical flows failed halfway through and showed raw code errors, which drove a steady stream of support tickets.

  • Drafting happened elsewhere

    The built-in editor was so limited that people wrote in Word offline, so Policy Manager stopped being the source of truth.

Two kinds of users

Editors
HR and compliance teams who draft and update policies every day.
Approvers
Board members and senior executives. They use the product the least and have little patience for complex software.

The approach

Five decisions that changed the product

I couldn’t rewrite the backend right away, so I started with what users felt first: the failures. Then I fixed the structure, and finally the roles.

Decision 1 of 5

Design for failure before redesigning the UI

The situation

The backend couldn’t be rewritten right away, and every crash showed raw red and yellow code errors in the middle of a task.

What I did

I replaced them with a standardized error library. With a UX writer borrowed from another team, I wrote messages that say what happened and what is safe. It didn’t fix the root cause, but every failure became a moment people could recover from while engineering stabilized the platform.

Why it mattered

Enterprise clients judge stability by what they see when something breaks. This was the fastest way to calm the accounts at risk and cut crash-related tickets.

Decision 2 of 5

Turn a local fix into a company standard

The situation

Auditing the errors showed the same gap across the whole Diligent suite.

What I did

I audited error, empty and 404 states across Diligent’s products, defined one standard and contributed the components to the central design system.

Why it mattered

Every team now solves these states once. Clients who use several Diligent products get the same behavior everywhere, which matters because Diligent sells them together as one platform.

Decision 3 of 5

Replace a fake wizard with flexible tabs

The situation

The review cycle was forced into a step-by-step wizard, but reviewers constantly move back and forth between a policy’s history and its draft.

What I did

I took the wizard apart and designed a tab-based layout where reviewers jump freely between the draft, its history and its references.

Why it mattered

Reviewing isn’t linear: people compare, go back, then approve. Tabs follow that and keep approval one step away, so review cycles don’t stall and clients stay audit-ready.

Decision 4 of 5

Integrate with Word instead of competing with it

The situation

Users already drafted in Microsoft Word and Google Docs. Building an editor good enough to replace them would have taken significant engineering resources.

What I did

I proposed an integration strategy and designed import and sync flows for Microsoft Word and Google Docs, and both shipped. Policy Manager kept a simpler built-in editor for quick edits, and heavy drafting stayed in the tools people already knew.

Why it mattered

Asking authors to switch was the real barrier. Meeting them in Word kept versions and the audit trail inside Policy Manager, avoided building a full editor, and let new clients bring their existing documents as they were.

Decision 5 of 5

Give approvers a shorter path

The situation

Many people only needed to review policies, but there were no roles and everyone got the full editor. Approvers were often board members and senior executives with little patience for complex software.

What I did

I defined roles with the team and designed a simplified review cycle for approvers: the stages, what’s due and their own tasks. HR and legal kept every review option. I first proposed a separate iPad app for board members; the team chose roles instead, so approvers got a lighter view inside the same product.

Why it mattered

Approvers use the product the least but can block the whole cycle. They’re also the most senior people at each client, so their experience shapes how the client sees the product at renewal time.

The redesign

A stable product that clients trust again

Policy Manager rebuilt on Lens, Diligent’s new design system.

Screen by screen

Two new capabilities

Sharing policies with subscribing school districts

Some clients are partner organizations that maintain policies for groups of school districts. As they moved from BoardDocs to Diligent Community, they needed to publish new or updated policies to those districts straight from Policy Manager, which made this feature critical to keeping them.

I designed Subscriber Management: admins add a district from the Community site, choose which policy folders it receives and manage its status.

Downloading a policy with its changes highlighted

Audits often ask for proof of what changed between versions. From the History viewer, users choose which versions to compare and download a PDF with additions and deletions highlighted, so they can share it with people who don’t use Policy Manager.

Still in the product today

Diligent’s public demo (2026) shows which decisions held up, and one that evolved in a way that confirms the reasoning behind them.

  • Decision 3 held up

    Review is still a tab next to the document, not a wizard, and the flow still carries the name of Lens, the design system it was built on. Reviewers keep moving between draft, history and references in any order.

  • Decision 4 held up

    Editing still happens in Word, inside Policy Manager, while versions and audit trail stay in the product. Diligent now sells the feature in those same terms. We shipped Word and Google Docs; the demo shows Word.

  • What evolved

    Creating a policy is still a three-step wizard, and that fits: creation is linear, review isn’t. It’s the same principle applied to a different task: match the structure to the way people actually work.

Looking back

  • What worked

    Fixing the experience of failure first bought the team time and trust while the backend was being stabilized.

  • What I’d do differently

    Track error rates and support tickets from day one, to show the impact of defensive design in numbers.

  • What I’d measure next

    How long approvers take to move a stage forward in the simplified review cycle, compared with the full one.
