Virtual office redesign for Edelap

Bringing bill payments back to Edelap’s own app

Edelap supplies electricity to about 350,000 customers in Greater La Plata, Argentina. Its virtual office was so confusing that many of them paid through third-party wallets instead. I audited it and redesigned it as a mobile app where paying directly is the first thing people see.

At a glance

The problem

The virtual office was so hard to read that “Pagar ahora” got about 1% of the attention, and many customers paid their bill through other apps.

What I did

I audited it with heatmaps, card sorting, a heuristic evaluation and interviews, then turned each finding into a mobile redesign.

The outcome

In usability tests, paying a bill went from 2 minutes to 40 seconds, and the payment success rate rose from 40% to 65%.

The question

Why were people paying Edelap’s bill somewhere else?

The old virtual office had usability and accessibility problems. Before redesigning anything, I wanted to know three things.

  1. 1Where in the journey do people get stuck?
  2. 2How can I measure what it costs them?
  3. 3Which fixes would change the most?

The business

The only power company in town still competes for the payment

Edelap is the only electricity distributor in La Plata and its area, so no one can take its customers. Other companies can take the moment the bill gets paid.

Customers can pay in Edelap’s own virtual office and app, but also through the Link and Banelco networks, by scanning the bill with a digital wallet, by phone, or in person at payment points. Every payment that goes through someone else’s channel costs Edelap more than one made in its own.

So making the app the easiest place to pay was a business goal. It lowers the cost of collecting and keeps people in the channel where everything else they need from Edelap already lives.

For design, that set the bar. Paying in the app had to take fewer steps than scanning the bill with a wallet, so the balance leads the home screen with the pay button right under it.

  • Collecting costs money

    Third-party collection networks charge for each payment they process, and the money can take days to arrive.

  • Payments arrive disconnected

    A payment made elsewhere has to be matched back to the account. Until it is, the bill can still look unpaid, and people call to ask why.

  • The rest goes unseen

    Someone paying in a wallet never sees the digital bill, automatic debit or the procedures they could do without visiting an office, each of which saves Edelap a visit, a call or a printed bill.

The research

Four methods pointed at the same problem

Each method looked at the virtual office from a different angle: where people looked, how they group things, which rules the interface broke, and what customers said.

Card sorting

The menu didn’t match how people group things

39 participants sorted 12 account features into 6 categories. Their groups didn’t match the existing navigation, which backed a full rework. Bills and payments was the clearest group: every participant put “Download invoice” there.

Heuristic evaluation

Six broken rules, two of them major

I reviewed the virtual office against Nielsen’s usability heuristics and rated each problem on his 0 to 4 severity scale: 2 major (3) and 4 minor (2). Each one became an input for the redesign.

  • Major

    Buttons with no size system

    Buttons vary a lot in size with no visual relationship, so it is hard to tell where you are on the screen.

    Supply screen · Consistency and standards

  • Major

    No primary action

    Every button uses the same color, so nothing stands out as the main action. Paying should look different from everything else.

    Supply screen · Aesthetic and minimalist design

  • Minor

    A button that looks like a title

    A button sits where a title is expected, with a different color and position from the others, which makes it almost invisible.

    Header and user summary · Consistency and standards

  • Minor

    Labels that look like a switch

    Two icons in a pill look like a toggle, but they are text labels. People already know that pattern as a switch.

    Housing type and digital invoice · Consistency and standards

  • Minor

    A button that adds nothing

    “Supply” opens a list of supplies that was already available in a dropdown on the previous screen.

    Supply and my supplies · Aesthetic and minimalist design

  • Minor

    “Join” doesn’t say what happens

    The label doesn’t explain what you are joining or committing to, so the outcome of the click is unclear.

    Join popup · Consistency and standards

Interviews

“I almost always end up lost”

Two quotes that sum up what people told me: they got lost, and they paid somewhere else.

  • “I rarely use the website because I almost always end up lost. I prefer other payment platforms.”

    Carolina · Edelap customer

  • “I don’t like the way they handle the app. It’s hard for me to find specific things. I usually use some other external app.”

    Alexander · Edelap customer

What I found

Four problems, four things to design for

  1. The information architecture is inconsistent.

    Organize the menu around the most used actions.

  2. The interface doesn’t follow common UI patterns.

    Make the main action obvious.

  3. The text uses words customers don’t recognize.

    Write in the words customers use.

  4. Type, color and shape have no hierarchy.

    Build a clear hierarchy of type, color and shape.

The biggest cut

From 11 menu items to 4

I cut the main menu to Inicio, Facturas, Reporte and Ayuda (Home, Bills, Report, Help), based on the card sorting results. Address and supply settings moved into the profile, since people rarely need them. Fewer options means people find what they need faster (Hick’s law).

The redesign

From paper to a home built around paying

From 1% of the attention to the main action

In the redesign, the balance and the payment button are the first things on the home screen.

After
1234

  • Nested settings: Address and supply management are nested within the profile to declutter the main interface.

  • Primary focus: The balance due is the clear visual focal point, supported by helpers for immediate status clarity.

  • Ergonomic button: A full-width button placed right below the balance: a large, predictable target that is quick to reach and tap (Fitts’s Law).

  • Balanced insights: The home shows a high-level YoY comparison, linking to detailed analytics for power users.

Looking back

What worked: combining heatmaps with card sorting showed both where people looked and how they expected the menu to be organized.

What I’d change today

  • Compare like with like

    The before numbers come from the desktop virtual office and the after numbers from the mobile prototype, so part of the change could come from the device. Today I’d test the old and new flows on the same device, with the same task.

  • Report who took part

    The results don’t say how many people took each test, so there’s no way to tell how solid a jump from 40% to 65% is. Today I’d publish the sample size next to every number and read small samples as a direction, not proof.

  • Put a number on the real problem

    The most important finding, that people paid through other apps, came from interviews and never got a number. Today I’d ask Edelap what share of payments happen outside its own channels and use that as the baseline.

  • Help people remember the bill

    I made paying faster, but nothing in the flow reminded people that a bill was due, or offered automatic debit after a successful payment. A bill paid automatically never leaks to another app. Today I’d add due-date reminders and offer automatic debit right after the first payment.

  • Let people name the groups first

    The card sort used six categories I defined, which checks a structure but doesn’t discover one. Today I’d start with an open sort and confirm the result with a tree test before high-fidelity screens.
