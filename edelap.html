Virtual office at Edelap

Bringing bill payments back to Edelap’s own app

Edelap supplies electricity to about 350,000 customers in Greater La Plata, Argentina. Its virtual office was so confusing that many of them paid through third-party wallets instead. I audited it and redesigned it as a mobile app where paying directly is the first thing people see.

Role
Product Designer
Type
UX audit and mobile redesign
Platform
Desktop to mobile
Methods
Heatmaps, card sorting, heuristic review, interviews

At a glance

The problem

The virtual office was so hard to read that “Pagar ahora” got about 1% of the attention, and many customers paid their bill through other apps.

What I did

I audited it with heatmaps, card sorting, a heuristic evaluation and interviews, then turned each finding into a mobile redesign.

The outcome

In usability tests, paying a bill went from 2 minutes to 40 seconds, and the payment success rate rose from 40% to 65%.

The challenge

The old virtual office had usability and accessibility problems. Before redesigning it, I wanted to know where users got stuck, how to measure it and which fixes would matter most.

  • Where in the journey do people get stuck?
  • How can I measure what it costs them?
  • Which fixes would change the most?

Heuristic evaluation

I audited the original virtual office against Nielsen’s usability heuristics and found 6 violations, rated on Nielsen’s 0 to 4 severity scale: 2 major (3) and 4 minor (2). Each one became a concrete input for the redesign.

Interviews

Two quotes that sum up what people told me: they got lost, and they paid somewhere else.

What I found

Each problem pointed to a design opportunity:

  1. Problem: The information architecture is inconsistent.

    Opportunity: Organize the menu around the most used actions.

  2. Problem: The interface doesn’t follow common UI patterns.

    Opportunity: Make the main action obvious

  3. Problem: The text uses words customers don’t recognize.

    Opportunity: Write in the words customers use.

  4. Problem: Type, color and shape have no hierarchy.

    Opportunity: Build a clear hierarchy of type, color and shape.

From 1% of the attention to the main action

In the original virtual office, “Pagar ahora” (Pay now) received about 1% of user attention. In the redesign, the balance and the payment button are the first things on the home screen.

Wireframes

Components

The components carry the audit’s fixes into the interface: one color for the primary action, consistent button sizes and a four-item navigation.

Fewer choices in the main navigation

The old virtual office had 11 items in its main menu. I cut it to four, Inicio, Facturas, Reporte and Ayuda (Home, Bills, Report, Help), based on the card sorting results. Address and supply settings moved into the profile, since users rarely need them. Fewer options means users find what they need faster (Hick's Law).

  • Nested Settings: Address and supply management are nested within the profile to declutter the main interface.

  • Primary Focus: The balance due is the clear visual focal point, supported by helpers for immediate status clarity.

  • Ergonomic CTA: A full-width button placed right below the balance: a large, predictable target that is quick to reach and tap (Fitts’s Law).

  • Balanced Insights: Home screen shows a high-level YoY comparison, linking to detailed analytics for power users.

Looking back

What worked: combining heatmaps with card sorting showed both where people looked and how they expected the menu to be organized.

What I’d change today

  • Compare like with like

    The before numbers come from the desktop virtual office and the after numbers from the mobile prototype, so part of the change could come from the device. Today I’d test the old and new flows on the same device, with the same task.

  • Report who took part

    The results don’t say how many people took each test, so there’s no way to tell how solid a jump from 40% to 65% is. Today I’d publish the sample size next to every number and read small samples as a direction, not proof.

  • Put a number on the real problem

    The most important finding, that people paid through other apps, came from interviews and never got a number. Today I’d ask Edelap what share of payments happen outside its own channels and use that as the baseline.

  • Let people name the groups first

    The card sort used six categories I defined, which checks a structure but doesn’t discover one. Today I’d start with an open sort and confirm the result with a tree test before high-fidelity screens.
